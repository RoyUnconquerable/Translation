# Style Guide

Target: a professional published English web-novel translation of a Chinese
xianxia serial. It should read as written in English, never decoded from
Chinese. When principles conflict, the order is: accuracy, then the natural
English sentence, then rhythm and voice.

## Voice and register

- Third person limited, measured and lightly literary, never fussy or
  archaic. Elevation comes from sentence shape and the more exact word,
  never the rarer word: take the plainer verb every time.
- Write in beats, not stacked structures. One assertion in spoken order:
  actor, action, consequence. Split a compound sentence at the comma-and
  when both halves stand alone. Avoid which-relatives; recast as a new
  sentence or a participle. Subject first; open with a connective (However,
  Thus, At that moment) only at a real logical turn, roughly one sentence
  in ten.
- Let rhythm serve the English: sentence length and paragraph texture may
  flex wherever the result sounds better and flows more naturally.
- Contractions by default, in narration as much as dialogue, but no
  contraction ending in 'd (write "he had", "I would").
- Register follows the speaker. Narration is direct and dignified. The
  protagonist's speech and inner voice are modern, quick, and may keep
  slang or mild cursing; never formalize or euphemize casual/internet
  diction. Dao Lords and formal speakers are courteous and composed
  (diction, not banned contractions); couplets and wenyan may go archaic.
- Be blunt where the source is blunt. Do not smooth a joke or a threat.
- Restate a name rather than lean on a bare pronoun when two same-gender
  referents share a paragraph. Generic persons take singular they; in
  scene-setting exposition the implied observer is "one", not "you".

## Tense

- Narration is past tense. Choose each tense deliberately: Chinese marks
  aspect, not tense, so the wrong choice silently rewrites the logic.
- Direct inner monologue is first person, present or future tense,
  unquoted, italicized with *asterisks*, punctuation inside the italics.
- Free indirect thought stays in plain roman past but breathes through its
  markers (Perhaps, after all, At worst); projections take plain
  would-conditionals, not rhetorical inversion.

## Dialogue conventions

- Straight ASCII double quotes only. Corner brackets 「」 and 『』 become
  straight double quotes; nested or scare quotes inside speech use single
  quotes. Terminal punctuation goes inside the closing quote.
- A source paragraph that is entirely a 【...】 line renders plain, Title
  Case, ending with a period: no bold, no brackets. An inline 【X】 inside
  prose likewise drops brackets and bold.
- Prefer action beat + quote in one paragraph, or a bare quote paragraph.
  Speech tags are rare; only said / asked / replied; a tag takes a comma
  before the quote.
- Hesitation keeps a leading ellipsis inside the quote ("...Hmph."). A
  silent response is the fixed form: Name: "..."
- Sound effects are CAPS, unquoted, on their own line: BOOM!
- Interrogative syntax takes "?" even when the raw ends with 。

## Honorifics and title policy

- Birth names are pinyin with diacritics intact (Lü Yang). Dao names,
  titles, and epithets are translated English.
- Honorifics translate: Fellow Daoist, Senior, Junior, True Person, True
  Monarch, Dao Lord. "Daoist", never "Taoist". 本座 = "This Seat".
- Capitalize He / His / Him / They / Them (and reflexives) only when the
  referent is a Dao Lord; a Dao Lord speaking of Himself capitalizes I /
  My / Me, and You / Your when a Dao Lord is addressed. A mortal's or
  disciple's grand pronouncement stays lowercase.
- Coined terms (techniques, realms, artifacts, cosmology) are roman Title
  Case in running prose: no italics, no brackets. Multi-word titles take
  attributive order ("Mountaintop Fire True Monarch", never "True Monarch
  of Mountaintop Fire"). Do not promote generic words into proper nouns:
  capitalize only what the glossary capitalizes.
- The glossary is the single term authority; one rendering per term, and
  disputes are resolved in the glossary, never improvised in the text.

## Number and unit policy

- Preserve every number, date, and measurement. Convert Chinese numerals
  to natural English numbers.
- Spell casual magnitudes (ten thousand years); use digits with commas for
  precise figures (129,600 years).
- Convert units idiomatically to miles / feet / inches / pounds; never li,
  zhang, jin, mu; avoid meters.
- American spelling throughout; "toward", never "towards"; "farther" for
  physical distance; serial comma always.

## Banned habits (translationese you never want)

- Em dashes, en dashes, curly quotes, and the single-glyph ellipsis (three
  periods instead). Semicolons: at most two per chapter, prefer zero.
- "and yet" - never; bare "yet" or "but".
- Comma splices in narration; split into two beats.
- Reflex fillers, budgeted per chapter: at most one each of "actually",
  "instantly", "at this moment"; at most two each of "suddenly", "simply";
  never reflex-write "couldn't help but".
- Calqued Chinese sentence frames: compose the English sentence, then check
  it against the source for meaning. Never translate the frame and furnish
  it ("pulled himself out of all that feeling" -> "shook off those
  emotions").
- Nominal stacks without a verb: a comma list with no predicate gets one;
  a trailing adjective tail moves forward or acquires a real predicate.
- Invented English idioms where the source is plain and technical; and,
  in the other direction, word-by-word calques of a chengyu that drop its
  figure. Keep the source's image inside a natural English frame; use an
  established English name for an allusion when one exists.
- Wrong collocations from vivid-verb reaching: risk is increased, not
  "sharpened"; a role is decisive; a stake is major.
- Imperative-conditional frames ("Set her against X and she still...");
  write the plain concessive or the past counterfactual.
- Translator notes, brackets, alternatives, or explanations inside the
  translated text. Concerns go in the closing report, never on the page.
- Repeating a distinctive phrase within a chapter, or a word within a
  paragraph, when the source did not repeat it on purpose.
